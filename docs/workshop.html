Community & regional carbon mapping · workshop

From soil cores to a carbon map you can defend

You bring cores. This workflow harmonizes them to standard depths, compares them with existing carbon maps and open data, and builds an updated estimate — or an updated map — at the resolution and precision you choose.

A · Harmonize

Bring every core to the same depth intervals so cores, studies and maps can be compared at all.

B · Compare

What do the existing maps say about your area, how sure are they, and do they agree with your cores?

C · Update

Combine cores and prior maps into a new estimate or map — simple areal extrapolation, or modelled — with honest uncertainty.

One example runs through everything.

A community samples a wetland area of interest (AOI). Year one: 3 cores. Year two: 13 more, placed where the first map said uncertainty was worst — 16 in all. A national carbon map covers the AOI, estimating 15 ± 3 kg C/m². Every number is fictional and chosen so the arithmetic stays clean; the same dataset ships with the code (data/example_soil_cores.csv) so R reproduces each result exactly. All units are kg C/m² for the 0–30 cm layer (1 kg C/m² = 10 Mg C/ha; that's the last time we'll mention the other unit).

Each section: the concept, the math, A the calculation by hand, B the same numbers in R from scratch, then where the workflow does it for real. If R and the hand calculation ever disagree, the hand calculation is right and the code has a bug.

Part A — Harmonize your cores
01

What we measure

How does a bag of wet soil become a number on a map?

A carbon stock is mass of carbon per unit of ground area, down to a stated depth. Three lab numbers per slice produce it: dry bulk density (how much soil per cm³), %C (what fraction of that soil is organic carbon), and the slice's thickness.

Stock of one slice stock = BD × %C⁄100 × thickness × 10  (g/cm³ · % · cm → kg C/m²)
AOne slice, by hand

The top slice of core EX-01: BD = 0.8 g/cm³, %C = 3.0, thickness 10 cm.

0.8 × 3.0/100        = 0.024 g C per cm³ of soil
0.024 × 10 cm        = 0.24  g C per cm² of ground
× 10 (unit change)   = 2.4 kg C/m²

The ×10 at the end is only units: 1 g/cm² = 10 kg/m².

BIn R
bd <- 0.8; pctC <- 3.0; thickness <- 10
bd * pctC/100 * thickness * 10
#> 2.4
Where the workflow does this The field workbook data/soil_carbon_calculation.xlsx (sheet 3) computes this per slice with every conversion visible — you fill the yellow cells, the grey ones calculate. It ships pre-filled with the example cores, so this exact 2.4 is its first row.
02

Harmonizing to standard depths

Your crew cut at 0–10–25–40 cm. The map reports 0–30. Now what?

Every survey slices differently, and nothing can be compared until all of it is expressed on the same intervals. The rule that keeps the translation honest: carbon is a mass — translation must not create or destroy it. A measured layer hands over carbon in proportion to how much of it falls inside the target interval, nothing more.

Overlap rule stocktarget = Σ stocklayer × overlap ⁄ thicknesslayer
ACore EX-01 to 0–15 and 15–30 cm
EX-01 as measured
Layer (cm)ThicknessStock (kg C/m²)
0 – 10102.4
10 – 25153.0
25 – 40151.8
Target 0–15:  all of 0–10          → 2.4
              5 of 15 cm of 10–25  → 3.0 × 5/15 = 1.0
              S(0–15)  = 3.4

Target 15–30: 10 of 15 cm of 10–25 → 3.0 × 10/15 = 2.0
              5 of 15 cm of 25–40  → 1.8 × 5/15  = 0.6
              S(15–30) = 2.6   →  S(0–30) = 6.0

Mass check: 3.4 + 2.6 + the untouched 30–40 remainder (1.8 × 10/15 = 1.2) = 7.2 = the whole core. Nothing created, nothing lost.

BIn R — 6 lines of base R
layers <- data.frame(top = c(0,10,25), bottom = c(10,25,40),
                     stock = c(2.4, 3.0, 1.8))
target <- c(0, 15)
# the depth range each layer shares with the target (0 if none)
overlap <- pmax(0, pmin(layers$bottom, target[2]) - pmax(layers$top, target[1]))
sum(layers$stock * overlap / (layers$bottom - layers$top))
#> 3.4     (target <- c(15,30) gives 2.6)
Where the workflow does this harmonize_core_depths() in R/step02_harmonize_depths.R — the same six lines looped over every core and every target interval (0–15, 15–30, 30–50, 50–100 by default), plus bookkeeping: coverage_frac records how much of each interval a core actually reached. Run scripts/run_02_harmonize_depths.R → outputs/soil_cores_harmonized.csv. Sheet 3 of the workbook does identical arithmetic, so spreadsheet and R check each other.
03

When a core stops short

The corer hit refusal at 25 cm. Is that a 0–30 cm observation?

Not by itself. Below the deepest measured layer the workflow can extend the profile with a simple exponential decline in carbon density — carbon usually thins with depth — anchored at the last thing actually measured. The honest rule: extrapolated carbon is kept in its own column, never silently mixed with measured carbon, and coverage_frac tells every later step how much was real.

Decay extension density(d) = ρbottom × e−k (d − zbottom)   k fitted per core; default 0.0587 /cm where unfittable
AEX-01, pretending it stopped at 25 cm
last measured layer 10–25: 3.0 kg/m² over 15 cm
ρ_bottom = 3.0 / 15 = 0.2 kg/m² per cm      k = 0.0587

extra(25→30) = (0.2/0.0587) × (1 − e^(−0.0587 × 5))
             = 3.41 × 0.254 = 0.87 kg C/m²

estimated S(0–30) = 2.4 + 3.0 + 0.87 = 6.27

The full core's true 0–30 value was 6.0 — the extension lands close (here ~4% high), but it is a model, not a measurement. That is exactly why it travels in a separate column.

BIn R
rho_b <- 3.0/15; k <- 0.0587
(rho_b / k) * (1 - exp(-k * 5))
#> 0.8665
Where the workflow does this Also harmonize_core_depths() — columns extrapolated_kg_m2, stock_total_kg_m2 and decay_k in its output. It fits k per core from the measured layers and falls back to the default when a core has one layer or gets denser with depth (common in peat).
Part B — Compare with existing data
04

What a prior map gives you

Someone already mapped carbon over your area. What exactly are they claiming?

A usable prior product provides two rasters: an estimate per pixel and an uncertainty per pixel. Averaged over the example AOI, the national map says 15 kg C/m², ± 3. Read the ± the way the makers meant it — some products publish a standard deviation, others a 90% interval width (divide by 3.29 to get an SD) — and note one distinction the workflow keeps for you: the average per-pixel uncertainty describes a typical pixel, which is not the same thing as the uncertainty of the AOI-wide mean.

The map's ±3 is your head start: it is information you did not have to sample for. Whether it can be trusted here is section 07's job.

Where the workflow does this data/prior_assets.csv is the registry — one row per product: Earth Engine ID, units, depth basis, and how its uncertainty layer should be read. scripts/run_00_data_inventory.R pulls every product over your AOI (clipped first, via rgee), reports coverage honestly (a peat-only map covering 40% of your AOI gets said out loud), and also checks the open core databases for existing ground data inside your AOI. scripts/run_01_characterize_prior.R summarizes the chosen prior. Existing packages do the lifting: rgee, terra, sf.
05

Your cores alone — the frequentist answer

Year one: three cores. What can classical statistics already say?

Before any Bayesian idea appears, ordinary statistics gives a complete, respectable answer from the cores alone: a mean, and a standard error saying how far that mean might sit from the truth. This is the baseline everything else must beat — and it is also where you should state your target before sampling: the margin of error you can tolerate (say ±20%), the confidence you want (say 80%), and — if you need a map — its resolution (say 30 m). All three are the user's choice, not the statistician's.

Mean, standard error, sample size SE = SD ⁄ √n   ·   margin of error = z × SE   ·   n needed = (z × SD ⁄ target)²
AThree cores and a calculator
year-one cores: 6, 15, 24        mean = 15
SD = 9   →   SE = 9/√3 = 5.2

at 80% confidence, z = 1.28:
margin = 1.28 × 5.2 = 6.7   →   15 ± 6.7  (±44%)

cores needed for ±20% (i.e. ±3.0):
n = (1.28 × 9 / 3.0)² = 14.8 → 15 cores

Three cores were never going to be enough — but now that's a number, not a feeling: ±44% today; ~15 cores to reach ±20% by sampling alone.

BIn R
x <- c(6, 15, 24); z <- qnorm(0.90)   # 80% two-sided -> 1.2816
c(mean = mean(x), se = sd(x)/sqrt(3), moe = z * sd(x)/sqrt(3))
#>  mean    se   moe
#>  15.0  5.196  6.66
ceiling((z * sd(x) / (0.20 * mean(x)))^2)
#> 15
Where the workflow does this Inside tier0_area_update() (R/step06_tier_ladder.R) — the cores' mean and SE are the "evidence" half of the update — and cores_needed_for_target() (R/step12_management_precision.R) is the sample-size formula with your margin and confidence as arguments.
Part C — Update: combine, map, decide, repeat
06

The Bayesian update

Two imperfect answers to one question. Why choose?

By year two there are 16 cores: mean 17, SD 8, so SE = 8/√16 = 2. Now two estimates of the same truth sit on the table — the map's 15 ± 3 and the cores' 17 ± 2. Neither is exact; each is a partial view of a true value lying underneath. That sentence is the Bayesian way of thinking. Discarding either wastes information; averaging 50/50 ignores that one is more trustworthy. The update does the sensible third thing: average them, weighted by confidence — and because two independent lines of evidence really are stronger than one, the combined answer is more certain than either input.

Normal–Normal update (precision = 1/σ²) 1/σ²post = 1/σ²prior + 1/σ²cores
μpost = σ²post × ( μprior/σ²prior + μcores/σ²cores )
AFive numbers and a calculator
prior 15 ± 3        cores 17 ± 2

precisions:   1/9 = 0.111      1/4 = 0.250
σ²_post = 1/(0.111 + 0.250) = 2.77   →  σ_post = 1.66
μ_post  = 2.77 × (15×0.111 + 17×0.250) = 16.4

16.4 ± 1.7 kg C/m²   (weights: cores 69%, map 31%)

Read it back: the answer leans toward the more precise voice, and 1.66 is smaller than both inputs. To reach ±1.66 by sampling alone you'd need (8/1.66)² ≈ 23 cores — sixteen cores plus a free map matched twenty-three without it. That is the efficiency the prior buys. (Year one worked too: 15 ± 5.2 ⊕ 15 ± 3 → 15 ± 2.6 — better than three cores alone, though still short of target, as section 10 will show.)

BIn R — the whole method is four operations
prior_m <- 15; prior_sd <- 3;  cores_m <- 17; cores_sd <- 2
p1 <- 1/prior_sd^2; p2 <- 1/cores_sd^2
post_var <- 1/(p1 + p2)
c(mean = post_var * (prior_m*p1 + cores_m*p2), sd = sqrt(post_var))
#>   mean     sd
#> 16.385  1.664
Where the workflow does this bayesian_update_normal() in R/step07_bayesian_update.R — these four lines plus one safeguard: where the prior map has no value, its precision is set to 0 so it goes silent rather than poisoning the result. tier0_area_update() wraps it with the cores' mean/SE and reports the weight the prior actually earned. Runner: scripts/run_06_model_ladder.R → outputs/tier0_area_update.rds.
07

Check before you trust: does the map agree with your cores?

The update assumed the map is honest here. Is it?

Two cheap checks, both using numbers you already have. Residuals: extract the map's value at each core location and subtract — a consistent sign means bias, not noise. Calibration: fit the line observed = a + b × map; a trustworthy map gives a ≈ 0 and b ≈ 1. If the slope's confidence interval excludes 1 — or worse, the map and your cores are uncorrelated — the map should be down-weighted (inflate its SD) or left out, and the update from section 06 falls back gracefully toward the cores-only answer. The update never forces you to trust a prior; this check tells you how much trust it earned.

BIn R — one line each
residuals <- observed - map_at_cores        # consistent sign = bias
summary(lm(observed ~ map_at_cores))        # want intercept~0, slope~1
cor.test(observed, map_at_cores)            # is there any relationship?
Where the workflow does this compare_prior_observed() (R/step04) computes the residuals, bias, MAE and RMSE; spatial_residual_diagnostics() (R/step05) maps them and — with enough points — tests for spatial pattern; tier1_calibration() (R/step06_tier_ladder.R) fits the calibration line and reports whether the slope's CI covers 1; prior_evidence_correlation() reports the correlation. All base R and stats — nothing exotic.
08

From one number to a map: the model ladder

A single AOI-wide value, or a 30 m surface? That depends on n, not on ambition.

Everything so far produced one number for the whole AOI — simple areal extrapolation, and for many management questions it is the right deliverable. Making a map means estimating how carbon varies from place to place, which takes more parameters and therefore more cores. The workflow arranges the options as a ladder, and a small helper counts your usable cores against each rung (~10 per parameter) and recommends a ceiling. You choose; it advises.

The ladder (details: docs/model_tier_ladder.md)
TierModelRough nProduces

3 spatial+ GP over x,y~80 locationsmean + SD maps

One rule with no exceptions. The prior enters once. Either it stays a prior (tiers 0–3 without it as a predictor, fused by the update) or it becomes a predictor inside the model (its fit then is the answer, and no further update happens). Doing both counts the same information twice and makes the map look more certain than it is.
Where the workflow does this tier_recommendation() prints the ceiling for your n; tiers 2–4 fit with brms (Bayesian regression via Stan — an existing, heavily-tested package, not custom samplers), compared with loo, each fit isolated in a clean subprocess by callr. Sampling diagnostics gate the comparison: a model that didn't converge can't win, whatever its score. Runner: scripts/run_06_model_ladder.R (tiers 2–4 behind FIT_BAYES <- TRUE).
09

The update at every pixel

Section 06 updated one number. A map is just that, everywhere.

Once a spatial tier provides evidence with a value and an uncertainty per pixel, the identical four-line update runs cell by cell: prior mean/SD raster ⊕ evidence mean/SD raster → posterior mean/SD raster. Where the prior map has no data (open water, a different ecosystem), its precision is zero there and the posterior is simply the evidence — no holes, no invented values. The resolution of the output grid is yours to set; honesty about what few cores can support at fine resolution comes from the SD map itself, which will say so loudly.

ATwo pixels by hand
pixel 1: prior 15±3, evidence 18±2  → 1/(1/9+1/4)=2.77
         μ = 2.77×(15/9+18/4) = 17.1 ± 1.7
pixel 2: prior NoData, evidence 18±2 → prior precision 0
         → posterior = 18 ± 2 (the evidence, exactly)
Where the workflow does this bayesian_update_raster() (R/step08) — the scalar formula in terra raster algebra. R/step09 exports the products (posterior mean, SD, and their ratio); R/step10 aggregates the posterior back to the prior's resolution as a sanity check; R/step11 maps what changed and where uncertainty shrank. Grid building and model-to-map prediction: R/step06_predict_grid.R (resolution = cellsize, your choice).
10

Good enough to manage by?

You set the bar before sampling. Did the estimate clear it?

The test is one comparison: the margin of error at your confidence level, against your tolerance. z encodes the confidence: 1.28 for 80%, 1.64 for 90%, 1.96 for 95% — all user-set, defaults are just defaults.

The test pass  ⇔  z × σpost ≤ margin × μpost
ABoth years of the example, target ±20% at 80%
Year 1:  15.0 ± 2.6  →  1.28 × 2.6 = 3.33 → 22.2% of 15
         FAIL (barely — and now you know by how much)

Year 2:  16.4 ± 1.7  →  1.28 × 1.66 = 2.13 → 13.0% of 16.4
         PASS

On a map the same test runs per pixel, and the share of pixels passing becomes the headline number.

Where the workflow does this test_management_precision() (R/step12_management_precision.R) — works on a single estimate or a whole raster with the same formula. Your two dials sit at the top of scripts/run_06_model_ladder.R and scripts/run_12_management_precision.R: TARGET_MARGIN and TARGET_CONFIDENCE.
11

Where to sample next year

Year one failed the test. What, exactly, do you do about it?

Two questions, both answerable from what you already computed. How many more cores? Invert the margin formula. Where? Wherever the posterior SD map is highest — sampling there buys the most certainty per core. This is the loop that makes the framework practical: sample what you can afford, update, map the remaining uncertainty, let it place next season's cores. Today's posterior is next year's prior.

APlanning at the end of year one
need SE ≤ margin × mean / z = 0.20 × 15 / 1.28 = 2.34

cores alone:      n = (SD/SE)² = (9/2.34)²          → 15 cores
with the prior:   evidence only needs the precision
                  the prior doesn't already supply:
                  n = 9² × (1/2.34² − 1/3²)          → 6 cores

The community collected 13 — more than 6, sensibly, because an SD estimated from three cores is itself shaky, and because the prior-assisted number leans on the map being unbiased (section 07's check). The efficiency story stays honest: the prior roughly halved the required effort; it didn't abolish it.

BIn R
se_t <- 0.20 * 15 / qnorm(0.90)
ceiling((9/se_t)^2)                      #> 15  (cores alone)
ceiling(9^2 * (1/se_t^2 - 1/3^2))        #> 6   (with the prior)
Where the workflow does this cores_needed_for_target() (R/step12) prints both numbers whenever the target isn't met, and propose_next_sample_sites() (R/step13) ranks map cells by posterior SD and returns coordinates for the next field visit.

Why Bayesian, in one paragraph

Frequentist statistics answers the cores-only question completely, and this workflow uses it for exactly that (sections 05, 07, 10). What it has no natural slot for is other evidence — the national map, last decade's survey, next door's cores. The Bayesian view supplies the slot: treat the true stock as an unknown that every dataset glimpses imperfectly, and combine the glimpses weighted by their stated uncertainty. Neither your cores nor the models are 100% right; combined honestly, they beat either alone — the estimate sharpens, the sampling effort shrinks, and what is still unknown gets mapped so it can direct the next season, rather than merely being confessed.
